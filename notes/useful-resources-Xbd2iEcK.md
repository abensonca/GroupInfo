### Online courses (Paul Menker's recommendations)

If any of you are like me, and are a physicist first, coder second, and you find yourself needing to learn coding better without doing a whole computer science degree, here are a few classes I've found helpful. Some of them can be kind of long, but oftentimes they can be useful even for one specific topic. Also, maybe I should say this, but I have logins for basically all of them that I'm happy to share:

* [Understanding Git and Github](https://www.udemy.com/course/git-and-github-bootcamp/) - pretty long, but watching the first few videos in any section should be a good overview
* [Python Masterclass](https://www.udemy.com/course/the-modern-python3-bootcamp/?couponCode=ST6MT103124) - anything you could possibly need to know about python short of building your own AI, but same advice applies here.
* [Writing "clean" code](https://www.udemy.com/course/writing-clean-code/) - maybe a little over the top, but can be helpful if everything feels sphagettified and impossible to debug
* [Faster python](https://www.linkedin.com/learning/faster-pandas/) - don't have access to this one anymore (but one can sign up for a free trial of LinkedIn Learning to watch it all for free). This one is technically geared towards the PANDAS module, but I think pretty much every idea can work with numpy as well
* [Writing tests](https://www.udemy.com/course/unit-testing-and-tdd-in-python/) - haven't finished this one yet, but it's a good introduction to the "veggies" of the coding world.

## Plotting & visualization

* [Paul Tol's colour-scheme notes](https://personal.sron.nl/~pault/) - colour-vision-deficient-friendly palettes designed for scientific plots; the qualitative, sequential, and diverging schemes are all worth knowing about.
* [ColorBrewer 2](https://colorbrewer2.org/) - palette picker for sequential / diverging / qualitative data; flags which schemes are colour-blind-safe and print-friendly.
* [Matplotlib gallery](https://matplotlib.org/stable/gallery/index.html) - searchable examples; usually the fastest way to find the snippet you need.
* "Points of View" - *Nature Methods*' short column on chart design; many quick reads on common pitfalls.

## Scientific writing & peer review

* *The Elements of Style* - Strunk & White; classic short book on prose, still useful for paper-writing.
* *How to Write a Lot* - Paul Silvia; on building a sustainable writing habit, especially for academics.
* "Ten simple rules for structuring papers" - Mensh & Kording, *PLOS Computational Biology* (2017); a good template that holds up in astronomy too.
* For peer review, skim the reviewer guidelines published by the journal you're reviewing for (MNRAS, ApJ, A&A all publish them). Local norms beyond that are mostly oral tradition - ask your advisor.

## Astronomy & cosmology references

* [NASA ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/) - the literature search engine; search by author, abstract, citations.
* [arXiv astro-ph](https://arxiv.org/list/astro-ph/new) - preprint server; daily listings are worth checking in your subfield.
* *Galaxy Formation and Evolution* - Mo, van den Bosch & White (2010); the standard graduate textbook for what most of our group works on.
* [Ned Wright's cosmology calculator](https://www.astro.ucla.edu/~wright/CosmoCalc.html) - quick redshift / distance / age conversions.

## Reproducibility & data management

* [Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/) - free archive for code and data with a DOI per release; the standard way to make a paper's code citable.
* [Astrophysics Source Code Library (ASCL)](https://ascl.net/) - registry for astronomy software (Galacticus is in there); good for finding existing tools and registering your own.
* "Ten simple rules for reproducible computational research" - Sandve et al., *PLOS Computational Biology* (2013); a concrete checklist-style guide.

## Giving talks

* *Trees, Maps, and Theorems* - Jean-luc Doumont; the most useful book on technical-talk and slide design out there.
* "How to Speak" - Patrick Winston's MIT lecture, freely available on MIT OpenCourseWare; ~60 minutes of concrete advice.
* For posters: treat it as a 60-second pitch with backup material; plan the one figure people will stop at, not the abstract.

## Career resources

* [AstroBetter](https://www.astrobetter.com/) - long-running blog covering academic-career topics in astronomy (jobs, software, productivity).
* [AAS Job Register](https://jobregister.aas.org/) - the standard astronomy jobs listing.
